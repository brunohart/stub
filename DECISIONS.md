# Decisions

Architecture decision records for Stub. Newest at the bottom. A decision is a decision until a later ADR says otherwise.

## ADR-001 — The heuristic parser is the floor, the model is measured against it

**Date:** 2026-09-06 · **Status:** decided

Stub reads ticket stubs with two parsers: a regular-expression parser built from cinema-ticket folklore (`HeuristicParser`) and the on-device language model through Foundation Models guided generation (`ModelParser`). The heuristic runs everywhere, always. The model runs only when `SystemLanguageModel.default.availability` is `.available`, and if it fails or returns no title, the heuristic result is used. Both write `readBy` and `confidence` onto the stub, and the raw Vision text is kept verbatim so a human can overrule either.

**Why.** On Day 0, in the iOS 26.2 simulator on macOS 26.6.2, the model reported itself available and then failed every request with `ModelManagerServices.ModelManagerError 1026`. Without the floor, the app would have had nothing. With it, all four fixtures were filed correctly. An app whose headline feature is on-device AI has to be honest about when the AI is not there, and useful anyway.

**Consequences.** The heuristic gets tests; the model gets an eval table (Day 3) that compares it to the heuristic on the same fixtures. The UI names which one did the reading.

## ADR-002 — The look is specified in SwiftUI first; Metal is an implementation, not the identity

**Date:** 2026-09-06 · **Status:** decided, Metal switch-over planned Day 1

The silkscreen treatment (desaturate, contrast, multiply onto parchment, grain), the misregistered orange plate, and the paper grain are defined as view modifiers in `Design/Paper.swift` and `Design/Type.swift`. Day 0 implements them with SwiftUI blend modes plus a Core Image noise plate. The Metal shaders in `Shaders/Silkscreen.metal` compile into the bundle and take over on Day 1 behind a switch.

**Why.** Apple's Metal toolchain catalog fetch failed twice on Day 0 (`Failed fetching catalog for assetType com.apple.MobileAsset.MetalToolchain`) before succeeding on the third attempt. The visual identity cannot depend on a download. Specifying the look at the modifier boundary means the implementation can change without the callers noticing.

## ADR-003 — Simulator first, with a seed path that exercises the real pipeline

**Date:** 2026-09-06 · **Status:** decided (the simulator is iOS 27 since ADR-014)

Every day's work must run in the iOS 26 simulator. Because the simulator has no camera and cannot be tapped from a script, Debug builds accept `-seed` (and `-reset`) launch arguments that push the bundled fixture stubs through Vision and the parsers and file them. `scripts/run.sh --seed --reset --shot …` is the daily proof.

**Why.** Bruno wants to iterate on design and interaction daily without a device in hand, and a scheduled build has no hands at all. Fixtures are synthetic tickets drawn by `scripts/make-fixtures.swift`, so they are honest inputs to OCR rather than hand-typed strings.

## ADR-004 — `project.yml` is the project; the `.xcodeproj` is a build artefact

**Date:** 2026-09-06 · **Status:** decided

The Xcode project is generated by XcodeGen from `project.yml` and is git-ignored. Targets, Info.plist keys, fonts and build settings are edited there.

**Why.** Diffs stay readable, merges stay possible, and an agent can add a widget target on Day 5 without touching a pbxproj by hand.

## ADR-005 — Private by construction

**Date:** 2026-09-06 · **Status:** decided

No network calls, no analytics, no account. Photos are stored in SwiftData with `.externalStorage`. Everything the reader saw is kept on the device and shown to the user on request.

**Why.** The product's one-line promise is "never uploaded". The architecture has to make that promise impossible to break by accident, not merely intended.

## ADR-006 — Two faces and a mono, numbers only in mono

**Date:** 2026-09-06 · **Status:** decided

Host Grotesk for words (Medium for display at −0.03em tracking), Newsreader for the single italic sentence and for reading, Fragment Mono for dates, seats, prices and confidence percentages. Hierarchy comes from size and space, not weight. The fonts ship as variable TTFs renamed without brackets (iOS did not register `HostGrotesk[wght].ttf` from `UIAppFonts`; `HostGrotesk-Variable.ttf` registers all six named instances). A Debug-only `FontAudit` logs what registered at launch.

**Why.** This is the designedbybruno type system, settled on 2026-09-03. The bracket finding cost twenty minutes and is recorded so it costs nothing next time.

## ADR-007 — Two detectors for the crop, and a quadrilateral that hugs the frame is not a ticket

**Date:** 2026-09-07 · **Status:** decided

`StubCrop` asks Vision's `DetectDocumentSegmentationRequest` first. If it finds nothing, or its quadrilateral touches three or more edges of the frame, `DetectRectanglesRequest` is asked next and its largest accepted rectangle wins. Both are straightened with `CIPerspectiveCorrection`. The full photograph is the floor, and `StubReader` falls back to it again if the crop reads as no text. The seed log and `StubReader.Result.detector` say which detector found the ticket.

**Why.** On the iOS 26 simulator on this Mac, segmentation returns the same full-width bottom-quarter strip for every fixture at 0.83–0.99 confidence, at every input scale, through both the new and the `VN` API. The same request on macOS finds the ticket to the pixel. This is the language-model failure again (ADR-001): a model that reports itself confident and is not there. The rectangle detector is arithmetic, not an asset; it found all four fixtures at 1.0, within a few pixels of the Mac's segmentation. Two detectors mean the crop works on the host the daily slot actually runs on, and the edge rule means a lying detector is caught by geometry rather than by luck.

**Consequences.** Do not remove the rectangle path when segmentation starts working on a device; keep both and let the log show which one is winning. Real photographs where the ticket fills the frame will fail the edge rule and fall to rectangles or the full frame, which is the ticket anyway.

## ADR-008 — Build products live outside `~/Documents`

**Date:** 2026-09-07 · **Status:** decided

`scripts/build.sh`, `test.sh` and `run.sh` use `~/Library/Developer/Xcode/DerivedData/Stub-scripts` as derived data (override with `STUB_DERIVED_DATA`). `build/` keeps only logs, test results and the slot markers.

**Why.** `~/Documents` on this Mac is an iCloud file-provider domain. The provider stamps every `.app` package under it with `com.apple.FinderInfo` and `com.apple.fileprovider.fpfs#P`, and `codesign` then refuses the bundle: "resource fork, Finder information, or similar detritus not allowed". Stripping with `xattr -cr` before signing lost the race every time, and a `.nosync` folder was tagged too. Day 1 started with the floor down for this reason.

**Consequences.** The repo can stay where Bruno keeps it. Anything that must be signed is built elsewhere. If the repo ever moves out of a file-provider domain, this can be reverted, but there is no reason to.

## ADR-009 — Two uneven columns stay; the table is the width of the screen

**Date:** 2026-09-08 · **Status:** decided

The table keeps its two columns at 1.15 : 0.85, overlapping by six points, the right column starting lower (DESIGN.md rule 4). The content stack inside the `ScrollView` carries `frame(maxWidth: .infinity, alignment: .leading)` so the columns are sized against the screen, not against the widest line of text above them.

**Why.** Day 1 asked whether landscape stubs at 2.3:1 want one wider column. The plates were small because the whole table was small: the `VStack` in the `ScrollView` took the width of its widest non-greedy child, the one-line season sentence, and `containerRelativeFrame` resolved against that. With the width restored the plates read at about 185pt and 137pt, the cinema names fit, and the zoom transition has a source worth growing from. A single column would trade the corkboard for a list.

**Consequences.** Anything laid out inside the table's scroll content must not depend on the sentence's length. The Debug `-drive` argument (`DebugDrive`) is the second honest stand-in for hands in the simulator after `-seed` (ADR-003); interaction days should extend it rather than add a third mechanism.

## ADR-010 — The model reads with the heuristic's draft in hand, and is scored in the drawer's shapes

**Date:** 2026-09-09 · **Status:** decided

`StubReader` runs `HeuristicParser` first, always, and passes its draft to `ModelParser` as a hint in the prompt ("a first pass by a rule-based reader: keep what the text confirms, correct what it does not, fill what it missed"). The model's answer streams (`streamResponse(to:generating:)`) and each snapshot is normalised through the same functions the heuristic uses (`normalisedScreen`, `normalisedSeat`, `stripFormats`, comma prices) before it reaches the fields or the eval table. Dates from the model are read as wall-clock time: any zone designator is dropped (`wallClock`). The eval harness (`StubTests/EvalTests.swift` → `docs/evals.md`) scores the heuristic, the cold model and the hinted model on the same eight fixtures, and asserts only the floor: the heuristic reads seven of eight titles and seats.

**Why.** Day 3 was the first day the model answered. Cold, on eight fixtures, it read 2/8 prices (it guesses USD for `$`), 7/8 dates (it swapped day and month once), 6/8 cinemas (it shortens "Event Cinemas Queen St" to "Queen St" and spelt "Roxxy"), and before normalisation it lost screens and seats it had read correctly by leaving them as printed ("SCR 2", "SEAT D 4"). Its first dates were all twelve or thirteen hours late because it wrote ISO 8601 with a `Z` for a ticket that prints the time on the wall of the cinema. With the heuristic's draft as a hint it read 8/8 on every field, at the same latency as cold (median 8.7 s against 8.9 s); the run before had it at 7/8 titles ("Aftrsun"), so it is not the same reader twice. The hint costs nothing and the correction is real, so the hinted model is the production path when the model is there. The heuristic is still the floor (ADR-001): it reads 8/8 on all six fields in about a millisecond.

**Consequences.** Never compare the model to the heuristic on raw strings; compare on what the drawer would file. Any new normalisation belongs in `ModelParser.draft(title:…)` so both columns of the table get it. A field the model reads better than the regex has to show up in `docs/evals.md` before the regex is loosened for it. The eval test takes about two minutes when the model is present and is the reason `scripts/test.sh` prints nothing for stretches; that is expected, not a hang.

## ADR-011 — The season is counted by hand and only phrased by the model

**Date:** 2026-09-10 · **Status:** decided

`SeasonSummary` counts the drawer (films, distinct cinemas, most visited, busiest month and weekday, total paid by currency) in a millisecond and writes `handSentence`. The on-device model is given only the summary's `brief` (one fact per line) and asked, through `@Generable struct Season`, for one sentence. Whatever it writes is judged by `SeasonRules` (under twenty words, no exclamation mark, no emoji, no digit, one sentence) and refused if it breaks one; the hand sentence stands until an accepted sentence arrives, and stands for good if none does. The sentence is cached against `SeasonSummary.key`, an FNV-1a hash of the brief, so the model is asked once per drawer, not once per launch, and the sheet's numbers are never the model's.

**Why.** The sentence is the one place the app is allowed a voice, and a language model is the right tool for a voice. It is the wrong tool for arithmetic and for restraint: the rules that make the line read as this app's ("dry, under twenty words, numbers spelled out") are the rules a model is most likely to break with enthusiasm. Instructions are a hope; a check on the way out is a rule. The hash is FNV over the brief rather than `Hashable` because Swift's hasher is seeded per process and a cache that forgets on relaunch is not a cache.

**Consequences.** New facts for the sentence go into `SeasonSummary` and its brief; they change the key and the sentence regenerates. `SeasonModel.update` waits for `StubReader.isBusy` to clear before asking, so a seed of eight stubs produces one sentence at the end and an import never queues behind it. Any new rule for the sentence is added to `SeasonRules.judge` and to the test, not only to the instructions.

## ADR-012 — One drawer in an App Group; the app writes it, the widget reads it, an intent runs inside the app

**Date:** 2026-09-11 · **Status:** decided

The SwiftData store lives at `SharedStore.url`: `Stub.store` inside the `group.com.designedbybruno.stub` container when the process has the group, otherwise the app's own Application Support. The app is the only writer. The widget (`StubWidget`) opens the same file with `allowsSave: false`, reads the most recently kept stub, and has a timeline of one entry with no schedule: `TableView` calls `WidgetCenter.reloadAllTimelines()` from the same `.task(id: summary.key)` that re-counts the season, so the widget is told exactly when the drawer changes and never polls. `LogStubIntent` and `FilmsThisYearIntent` run in the app's process (there is no intents extension); `Reach` is the bridge, lent the app's container at launch so the count is read from the open drawer rather than a second connection to the same file. A drawer kept before Day 5 at SwiftData's default path is moved into the group once, on the first launch that knows about it (`migrateIfNeeded`), and never overwrites a shared store that already exists.

**Why.** The widget needs the drawer, and two processes writing one SQLite file through two SwiftData stacks is a race the app cannot referee. One writer and a read-only reader that is told when to look is the shape that cannot corrupt anything. The fallback to Application Support keeps ADR-003 honest: a host without the group (a simulator build without the entitlement, a device profile without the group) still runs the app in full; only the widget goes empty, and the seed log says `Store: app-local, no App Group` so nobody has to guess.

**Consequences.** The widget compiles `Stub.swift`, `Ink.swift`, `Type.swift` and `SharedStore.swift` from the app's tree; nothing in those four files may import UIKit, Metal or Foundation Models (the misregistered plate moved to `DisplayTitle.swift` for this reason). Anything that changes the drawer without going through the table's task (a future delete from the detail, a camera import that files straight to the store) must call `Reach.refreshWidgets()` itself. The widget shows text only: the plate's `imageData` is external storage and a photograph in a small widget would be grit in the wrong place. If a second widget or a Live Activity ever wants to write, it goes through an intent that runs in the app, not through the store.

## ADR-013 — The table is two columns until the type gets large; at the accessibility sizes it is one

**Date:** 2026-09-12 · **Status:** decided (amends ADR-009 for the accessibility sizes only)

`StubTable` keeps its two uneven columns (ADR-009) at every standard Dynamic Type size, up to and including xxxLarge. At the five accessibility sizes (`DynamicTypeSize.isAccessibilitySize`) it lays the stubs in one column, full width, still tilted, still overlapping nothing. Titles are never truncated at any size: the card's `lineLimit(2)` is gone and the title takes the lines it needs. The detail's row of four figures becomes a stack at the same threshold. Every card is one VoiceOver element that says the film, the date in words, the cinema and the seat, is announced as a button, and opens on double-tap through `accessibilityAction`, because the press gesture keeps it from being a `Button`.

**Why.** At AX5 the 0.85 column is about 150 points wide and a 17-point face is drawn at roughly 40; "Dune Part Two" is a word a line and the corkboard is a ransom note. The designedbybruno rules say the grid shifts to a single column when the metaphor changes from a table to a stack of cards in the hand; a person reading at the accessibility sizes is holding the phone that close. Truncating a title to keep a column narrow would be the type paying for the layout, and DESIGN.md says the type is constant and quiet. The threshold is the platform's own, not a width measured by hand.

**Consequences.** Nothing new is laid out in the table that assumes two columns. The one-column table is proved by a screenshot at an accessibility size (`scripts/run.sh --type accessibility-extra-large`), the two-column table at xxLarge. A future wide-column decision for landscape stubs (Day 1's question, answered on Day 2) is separate from this.

## ADR-014 — The simulator is iOS 27; the app still targets iOS 26

**Date:** 2026-09-26 · **Status:** decided (amends ADR-003 for the simulator only)

The daily proof runs on an iPhone 18 Pro on the iOS 27 runtime: `scripts/sim.sh` looks for a booted iOS 27 iPhone and otherwise boots the newest iPhone 18 Pro. `IPHONEOS_DEPLOYMENT_TARGET` stays `26.0`, and every `#available(iOS 26.0, *)` gate stays where it is.

**Why.** Xcode 27 arrived and the host's iOS 17.5–26.5 simulator runtimes went with the old one; the iPhone 17 Pro on iOS 26 that `sim.sh` asked for no longer exists, so the daily slot would have stopped before building. Following the toolchain costs one line (`Paper.swift`'s multiply overlay, which the iOS 27 SDK finds ambiguous between `.overlay`'s view and shape-style overloads) and keeps the floor where the next slot will actually run. Raising the deployment target would drop every phone still on iOS 26 for no feature the app uses.

**Consequences.** On this host the on-device model does not answer on the iOS 27 runtime (the probe says it is still downloading), so the simulator now proves the heuristic path (ADR-001) and not the model's; `docs/evals.md` keeps the 2026-09-12 run from the 26.5 runtime until a model run replaces it. The eval report names the runtime it ran on rather than assuming one. Nothing is proved on iOS 26 any more unless that runtime is reinstalled (Xcode › Settings › Components).

## ADR-015 — Every release gets an edition: the hash draws it, the model art-directs it from a closed vocabulary

**Date:** 2026-09-27 · **Status:** decided; written without a Swift toolchain, unproved in the simulator until the next Mac slot builds it (see `docs/LOG.md`)

A kept stub comes back as two things. The first is the stub as scanned: the photograph, straightened, the archive, exactly what it was before (ADR-005, ADR-007). The second is an **edition**: a ticket designed for that film's release, printed with this stub's own seat, date, cinema and screen, and which viewing of the film it was. Every stub of the same release shares the edition's design; every copy is its own.

An edition is chosen from a closed vocabulary and drawn by code. Eight movements (`swiss`, `constructivist`, `deco`, `cutout`, `riso`, `letterpress`, `blueprint`, `noir`), twelve palettes, four stocks (`cotton`, `coated`, `foil`, `holographic`) and a composition seed. `Release.key` folds a title to the one name its editions share (formats stripped, diacritics and punctuation folded, lower case); its FNV-1a hash seeds a SplitMix64 generator (`Dice`) that picks the floor edition and every composition choice under it: the angles, the positions, the counts, where the title breaks. When the on-device model is there, it is asked once per release, with the title, the year and the screen, to choose the movement, the palette and the stock through `@Guide(.anyOf(…))`. Its choice is judged on the way out (`EditionRules`) and cached against the release key (`EditionCache`). The composition seed is never the model's.

The edition is lit by one light. The phone's attitude (Core Motion) or a finger's drag tilts the card and moves the light; Metal draws the ink pressed into the stock (`relief`), the foil and the holographic film (`foil`) and the stock's own surface (`stock`), all from that one light. Core Haptics plays the stock under a moving finger and clicks once at the perforation. The detail's hero is the edition, and turning it over shows the stub as scanned, mounted on its back. After Keep, the edition is printed pass by pass, one plate at a time, the foil last.

**Why.** The model is a good judge of a film's tone and a bad designer. The only safe way to let it near the look is to let it choose between things that are already designed: a closed vocabulary means an edition can never be ugly in a new way, and a rule checked in code means an answer outside it is refused rather than drawn. The hash means there is always an edition, on any phone, before the model answers and when it never does; that is ADR-001's floor again, for taste instead of text. Keying on the release rather than the stub makes the edition something you share with everyone who saw that film, and the viewing number makes your copy yours. The tilt, the light and the haptics are there because a ticket is an object you turn over in your hand; the app already treats stubs as objects on a table (DESIGN.md), and the edition is the first one you can pick up.

**Consequences.** The hash picks from the lists as they stand at `Genome.version`. Growing the vocabulary means a new version, so that an edition already printed never reprints itself; a test pins the floor for three titles. The model's vocabulary and the genome's lists are the same literals, and a test says so. The edition is the one place the app prints in inks other than its own; the app's chrome keeps `Ink`. The faces stay three (ADR-006); inside an edition weight is allowed, because an edition is a poster and not the app's chrome. Nothing on an edition is invented: no tagline, no plot, no stars; the Aztec code on its back encodes exactly what its strip prints. The gyro is not started under Reduce Motion, and the edition lies flat until a finger tilts it. The simulator has no gyro, so `-tilt x,y` holds the light where a screenshot wants it, and `-edition` opens the first stub's edition after the seed (ADR-009: extend `DebugDrive`, do not add a third mechanism).

## ADR-016 — The press: a proof is a diff over the edition, and every part has its own die

**Date:** 2026-09-28 · **Status:** decided; the data (Day 23) is built and tested, the press room is Days 24–27 (`docs/briefs/the-press.md`)

The person who kept a stub gets the power the model has (ADR-015) and one more: for any part of the poster, they can ask for another drawing of that part and keep the one they like. They choose between drawings. They never move a mark: no colour picker, no drag, no text field. Every choice stays inside the closed vocabulary and the seeded composition, so an edition can never become ugly in a new way. ADR-015's reason now covers a third director.

**Parts.** Every movement is split into named parts (`Part`, `Movement.parts`), declared in the order they are drawn and namespaced by movement (`"constructivist/disc"`). Every poster `Mark` carries its part; the strip's marks carry none, because the strip prints facts and facts have no takes. A part is a **frame** (the geometry the others hang from, at most one a movement), a **piece**, or **set** (it rolls nothing: set from the copy, the frame or the movement's rules; it can be touched and explained but not turned). The dependency rule: a frame reads only its own die; a piece reads only its own die and, if it hangs, the frame's outputs. So turning a piece moves that piece and nothing else, and turning the frame moves only what hangs from it. `PressTests.partsAreIndependent` holds all eight movements to it, over three copies and twenty takes of every part, and fails when one part's die leaks into another (checked by breaking it on purpose). Frames exist where something actually hangs: swiss's grid, constructivist's diagonal, deco's sunburst, blueprint's circle. The brief also proposed frames for cutout's field, riso's screen, letterpress's margins and noir's blind, but in the drawings nothing hangs from those: they are pieces (screen, blind) or set (field, margins, night), and those four movements have no frame.

**Takes.** A take is an integer per part, 0 to 99. Take 0 is the part's own die, `Dice(seed).fork(part id)`: the drawing that comes from the title. Take *n* is that die forked again by `"take n"`. `Edition.dice(_:take:)` is the only function that builds a part's die.

**The proof.** `Proof` holds what differs from the edition underneath: an optional movement, palette and stock from the genome's lists, the takes keyed by namespaced part id (so a trip to riso and back keeps the constructivist takes), when it was pulled, and the version it was pulled against. It is JSON in `UserDefaults` (`ProofCache`, one dictionary keyed by release, never pruned; the privacy manifest's CA92.1 already covers it). The same proof prints the same card on every phone. A real one is about 120 bytes, not the brief's sixty: the namespaced ids and the date cost the difference, and both are worth it.

**One seam.** `Editions.edition(for:)` returns the printed edition with its pulled proof applied (`Edition.applying`). The detail, the print run and the share get the proof without knowing it exists. An applied proof that differs in anything is `directedBy: .you`; one that differs in nothing gives back the edition exactly, and pulling it is going back. The edition underneath is never overwritten and keeps its own director, so **going back is deleting the proof**. The detail shows "What the press keeps": the proof's JSON and its size.

**Version 2, and why version 1 could be retired.** Splitting the movements into parts changed every composition, so `Genome.version` is 2. `Genome.floor` did not change (it forks `"genome"`), so every release kept its movement, palette and stock; `floorIsPinned` changed only its version line. ADR-015 says an edition already printed never reprints itself. Version 1 was retired rather than frozen because Stub has never shipped: no TestFlight, one phone. A cached v1 edition is redrawn at v2 on first sight, keeping who chose it, with a log line saying so. Once the app ships, a change like this needs a frozen path for the old version, drawn by the old code. The v2 goldens (`PressTests.goldensMatchTheMirror`) were computed by `docs/editions/edition.js` under Node (`docs/editions/golden.js`), which rolls the same dice in the same order as the Swift and moves in the same commit.

**In-betweens (Day 24).** The wheel draws the space between two takes: marks paired by part and index interpolate, anything else cross-fades. Only the part being turned changes, so only it moves; the rest of the poster stands still. That is the dependency rule, made visible.

**One exception to "nothing moves on its own" (Day 27).** After a pull the new ink is wet for about two seconds: a gloss that follows the one light and dries away. It is the consequence of an act, it ends, and the timeline driving it stops at zero. Nothing else in the press moves unless a finger moves it.

**Consequences.** A new part, or a new roll inside a part, is a composition change: it lands in the Swift and in `edition.js` together, and it moves the goldens, which is a new version. A part whose marks read another piece's outputs breaks the rule and the test; make the shared geometry the frame or make it set. The model never touches composition and the person never touches coordinates.

## ADR-017 — No grain: the parchment is flat, and so is everything printed on it

**Date:** 2026-09-28 · **Status:** decided (amends DESIGN.md rule 2 and Editions rule 3)

Stub had grain everywhere: per-pixel noise at 6% over the parchment behind every screen, a breath of it in the silkscreen pass over every stub photograph, a noise plate over the blank stub and the blank card, per-pixel speckle in every edition's stock, and 26,000 specks on the icon. All of it is gone. The parchment is `Ink.paper`, flat. The silkscreen keeps what makes it a print (the desaturation, the contrast, the multiply against the paper, the misregistered plate) and loses the noise. The edition's stock keeps what makes it a material: cotton's soft fibre (low-frequency value noise, the difference between rag and coated card), the sheen that follows the one light, the relief, and the brushed lines on metal foil. `Grain`, `GrainPlate` and the `paper` shader are deleted; `silkscreened` takes only a strength.

**Why.** Bruno's call on seeing it in the app: the grain reads as fake grain, and that is not a nice design for an app. Grain is a print cue borrowed for screens, and on a phone, over everything, at every size, it reads as a texture laid on top rather than a quality of the paper. The warmth was always the colour. The designedbybruno taste doc's paper-grain signature is set aside for this app.

**Consequences.** Do not add noise overlays, grain plates or per-pixel hash speckle to any surface, chrome or edition. A material that needs a texture gets one that is a property of the material and responds to the light (fibre, sheen, relief, brush), not speckle. `docs/editions/lit.js` mirrors the stock shader without grain. The day-by-day screenshots before this date show the grain; they are a record, not a spec.


## ADR-018 — The room's light: on a real table, the card is lit by the room

**Date:** 2026-09-29 · **Status:** decided (amends DESIGN.md › Editions rule 4 for this one view); the entity is built and tested in the simulator, placing it is a device test (`docs/device.md` §3.9). The brief called this ADR-017; that number went to taking the grain out.

"Put it on the table" in the detail sets the card, at its real size, on a real surface through the camera (`RoomView`). The card is an entity (`RoomCard`): `TicketShape` extruded to four tenths of a millimetre (`MeshResource(extruding:)`), so the corners, the notches, the perforation and the punches are cut through the paper, with a plane on each face. Everything the phone's light did in Metal, the room's light does here through RealityKit's physically based material, from maps drawn once by `ImageRenderer` from the same composition the detail draws: the card printed unlit for the base colour (`editionLit` off: the stock without its sheen, no relief, the foil flat in its metal), the foil's shape for metallic, the stock's roughness with the foil smooth, the inks as a height field for the normal map (the same height as `edition_height`, the same wall width as `relief`), and the card's own alpha, cut at a half, for opacity. Holographic film is `holographic` in `Room.metal`, a RealityKit surface shader carrying `foil`'s thin film, driven by the real view direction. The session is `ARWorldTrackingConfiguration` with horizontal planes and `environmentTexturing = .automatic`, so the foil reflects the room; the card sits on the first table big enough, a tap sets it down elsewhere, and a finger moves and turns it. The coaching overlay is the system's.

**Why.** DESIGN.md says one light, and it is the phone's, because a card tilting in a hand has nowhere else to get its light from. On a table the card has a room around it, and a light that ignored the room would be the one fake thing in the view. The maps are drawn from the composition and not re-lit in Metal because RealityKit's material is what knows the room's light (environment texturing, the directional estimate), and asking both to light the card would light it twice. `ARView` is used rather than `RealityView` because environment texturing and the coaching overlay are its own, known and stable on iOS 26; the representable is small.

**Consequences.** The Room is gated on `ARWorldTrackingConfiguration.isSupported`: the simulator and phones without world tracking never see "Put it on the table". In Debug, `-room` opens it on any host with a stand-in (no camera, a parchment table, one directional light from the upper left, the default image-based light turned down), which is how the simulator proves that the entity builds; the stand-in is a proof, not a feature. Nothing leaves the phone: the camera feeds ARKit, nothing is recorded, and the usage string says both uses. A new surface on the card (a new stock, the patina's foxing) reaches the room only if it reaches a map; the patina does, through the stock shader in the base colour, as long as `ImageRenderer` draws shaders (Day 30 checks). The card's size is `RoomCard.metres`, 64 mm across; nothing scales it to the room.

## ADR-021 — Add to Stub: a ticket handed over in a link opens the sheet; it never files itself

The app is associated with the domain (`applinks:designedbybruno.net`; a Debug build adds `?mode=developer`, so the simulator reads the association file from the site rather than waiting a day for Apple's CDN), and `TableView.onOpenURL` hands the URL to `Reach.open`. `AddLink.read` reads each field on its own: words cleaned and cut at 200 characters, `at` as ISO 8601 with its offset (the phone's zone when there is none, midnight for a date alone), a price as digits only, a currency as three letters. The association file names the team and the bundle (`7CLZHA268P.com.designedbybruno.stub`); if either changes, the file on the site changes with it.

## ADR-022 — The home names itself; every way somewhere is a named control; buttons are stamps; things are put down

**Date:** 2026-10-13 · **Status:** decided (amends DESIGN.md rule 1, The press rule 5 and BEDIP-221's mark setting; adds rules 10 to 13). Built and screenshotted in the simulator; how the stamps, the deal and the plates feel under a thumb is a device test (`docs/device.md` §3.5 and §3.8).

Bruno's calls on using the app, in one afternoon: the way to its functions was not obvious; the italic was doing navigation's work badly; "Stub" over an italic sentence took the home screen's identity away; the buttons and transitions had no kick of their own; the moving share should always say where it came from, with no option not to; the icon needed craft; the fan closed on every movement, so movements could not be compared; the plates' type trailed as they parted and closed; and it was not clear the poster could be touched and turned with the dial.

**The home names itself.** The table's header is a brass label holder of the kind screwed to a card-catalogue drawer, with a typed card in it: the years the drawer spans in the mono, and how many stubs and cinemas (`DrawerLabel`). Its sheen follows the phone's tilt as the edition's foil does; it stays put under Reduce Motion and in the simulator. Tapping it opens the season. The app's name and the season's sentence are gone from the home; the sentence lives on the season sheet. The empty drawer keeps its one sentence, in Newsreader roman.

**Named controls.** The drawer's two doors are a dock at the bottom of the table: The season (with how many films) and Keep a stub. Under the card in the detail is a row of paper stamps: The press, Share and, on a phone that can find a table, On the table. Turning a card over is the card's own gesture, not a button; the line under the card says "Tap the card to turn it over" until the person has done it once (`taught.turn`). In the press room every part that turns wears printer's registration marks at its corners, in registration (ink over a paper halo, so they read on any palette), put down one after another as the room opens and then resting fainter; the line under the card says "Tap a part of the poster, then turn the dial" whenever nothing is in hand; the lever says Pull, in orange once there is something to pull. The bench's choices are stamps, the open one inked. The import sheet's Keep is a full-width stamp at the bottom, faint until there is a title.

**The italic is the voice.** Newsreader italic says one kind of thing: the season, on its sheet, and what an edition prints. Every instruction and status that had borrowed it is Host Grotesk now; the press's take is a number in the mono ("The disc  take 13").

**Stamps.** `StampStyle` is every button on the parchment. The ink sits three points out of register with a plate (orange under ink, navy under paper). Pressed, it goes down onto the plate inside two frames (`Motion.touchDown`), squashed a breath under the hand, and prints in register with one rigid haptic at contact. Released, it springs back with a bounce, lifts a couple of points past rest tipped a degree as a hand tips a stamp coming off the paper, settles on SwiftUI's bouncy spring, leaves a ring of ink spreading and fading where it printed, and is felt once more, softly. Words alone dip and fade. Under Reduce Motion nothing travels. Toolbar items on parchment have no shared glass.

**Things are put down.** On the first open the cards are dealt onto the table, newest first, 55 ms apart, a beat after the table is there, each falling from a little above and a few degrees further turned, from alternate sides, with `Motion.drop`; a kept stub is dropped the same way. The season's figures are put down in turn. The launch screen is the parchment, so nothing fades in from white.

**The press, in the hand.** A movement chosen from the fan flies to the bed and prints, and the fan stays open, so movements can be compared one after another; the inked Movement stamp closes it. The bed draws the card as its stack of sheets even at rest (`EditionFace.asSheets`, the sheets a ten-thousandth apart, since SwiftUI skips an identity projection): the flat card and the projected sheets are different rasterisations, and switching between them as the plates began to part, inside a spring that also cross-faded the two, made every edge of the type shimmer and trail. A test holds the first frame of parting to its own sub-pixel motion and measures the old switch at the same threshold. The pinch hands its speed to the spring (`interpolatingSpring` with the pinch's velocity), so a flick carries on and a slow pinch settles; the status words and the button's label change at once rather than cross-fading under the plates.

**The watermark.** Every share carries "shared in Stub" beside a small ticket, blind-embossed into the parchment under the card (`Watermark`): no ink but a shade of the paper, read by its relief, lit on the edges that face the light and shaded on the others, and in the moving share the relief follows the moving light. It is on the vertical clip, the close-up clip and the still. There is no setting to leave it off (Bruno's call; BEDIP-221 had proposed one). The share's text carries designedbybruno.net/stub.

**The icon.** A single ticket stub, kept: cream card torn along its perforation (half of every hole, the paper ragged between), ADMIT ONE letterpressed in ink with the orange plate a pass out of register, Nº 0001 in the mono, a double rule, a crescent conductor's punch through it showing the table and the card's thickness in its wall, lying turned on a dark table under a lamp with a wide soft shadow and a tight contact shadow. Drawn by `scripts/make-icon.swift` from a fixed seed; it reads at 60 points as a cream ticket on dark.

**Why.** DESIGN.md's rule 9 says remove; it does not say hide. A card you can hold, turn, press, share and set on a table was showing one of those things, and italic sentences pointing at the others read as prose. A control that says what it does is rule 7 (honest machinery) applied to navigation. The stamp and the deal are the app's own physics rather than the platform's: pressing a button is printing a pass, which every other surface in Stub already pretends to have been. The label is the home's identity because the home is a drawer, and a drawer is named by what it holds, not by the brand. The watermark is how a share becomes an invitation without a logo slab: it is made of the same paper as everything else.

**Consequences.** A new act on a card goes in the detail's row, not in a gesture alone and not in an italic hint. A new button on the parchment is a `StampStyle`: ink for a screen's one primary act, paper for the rest, quiet for words. The italic is not used for anything a person is meant to do. A new share format carries the watermark. Anything that animates the plates must keep the bed on the sheets path. The day-by-day screenshots before this date show the old chrome and the old icon; they are a record, not a spec.

## ADR-023 — Tickets that never had paper: the share extension leaves them in an inbox; a pass is read from its fields, a PDF from its text

**Date:** 2026-10-13 · **Status:** decided; built and proved in the simulator (a screenshot shared from Photos through the extension, and a pass and a PDF through the inbox), sharing a real Wallet pass is a device test (`docs/device.md` §3.12). BEDIP-218.

`StubShare` is a share extension for exactly one item that is a Wallet pass (`com.apple.pkpass`), a PDF or a picture. It shows what it was handed, as Stub will read it, and one stamp, Send to Stub; sending copies the file into `Inbox/` in the App Group container with a note beside it (`Inbox.put`) and the extension closes. It compiles only what that needs: the inbox, the ZIP and pass readers, the PDF page, the draft, the inks, the faces and the stamps. No store, no Vision, no model, no Metal. The app takes the inbox, oldest first, every time it comes to the front (`Reach.drainInbox`), and each item becomes an `Arrival` on the import sheet (ADR-021): nothing is filed until the person keeps it there, where its edition prints.

**A pass is read, not looked at.** `Zip` reads just enough of the format to open a `.pkpass` (the central directory, stored and deflated entries through `Compression`, every size checked before anything is allocated, ZIP64 and encryption refused). `WalletPass` reads `pass.json` in this order for each of the ticket's fields: PassKit's semantic tags (`eventName`, `venueName`, `eventStartDate`, `venueRoom`, `seats`, `totalPrice`), which say exactly what they are; then the visible fields by key or label, in English or French ("Film", "Session", "Seat" and "Row" together, "Salle", "Place"; a "Cinema" field holding "Cinema 4" is a screen, not a building); then `relevantDate` for the time and `organizationName` for the cinema. What none of those settles goes to the heuristic parser, run over the pass's own words in the app. A pass that stated everything it has is filed at full confidence, read by `wallet-pass`; one the heuristic had to help is 0.8. Its plate is its strip (else its background, else its thumbnail), silkscreened like any stub; never its logo. The signature is not checked: Stub is not Wallet, the pass has already been added to Wallet or not, and a redacted pass would fail it.

**A PDF is read from its own text.** A booking system's e-ticket is set in real type, so `PageImage.text` gives the first page's words as written and they go straight to the parsers, as a camera's live text does; the page, drawn on white at two pixels a point, is the plate, cropped to the ticket. Only a PDF with no text of its own (a scan) is read by Vision, and then the whole page. A screenshot is read as a photograph is: cropped to the ticket, then Vision.

**Why.** Most tickets now arrive as a pass, a PDF or a QR screen, and until Stub took those it was a paper archive. The inbox keeps ADR-012's one writer: a second process writing the SwiftData store would be a second history for the app (and, with ADR-024's sync, for CloudKit) to merge, and the extension's memory limit is no place for Vision and a language model. Reading a pass's fields rather than its picture is the honest machinery rule: the pass states the seat, so nothing should guess it. The first PDF test proved the text rule: reading the rendered page cropped to the bordered ticket lost the cinema in the heading above it, and reading the whole page let OCR garble the terms into a title; the PDF's own text read every field right.

**Consequences.** The extension never opens the store and never asks the model. A new kind of shared ticket is a new `Inbox.Kind`, a branch in `drainInbox` and an `Arrival` content; the import sheet stays the one place a ticket is kept. The fixtures in `fixtures/passes` are synthetic, three shapes of Apple's format made by `scripts/make-digital.swift`; real passes, redacted, belong beside them, and a circuit whose pass reads wrong gets its labels added to `WalletPass.Field.words` with a test. The eval reads the PDF and the screenshot as the app does (`docs/evals.md`).

## ADR-024 — The drawer outlives the phone: the person's private iCloud, on by default; uniqueness in the app; a venue and a circuit for every stub

**Date:** 2026-10-13 · **Status:** decided; the schema, the migration, the ledger, the setting and the venues are built and tested in the simulator. Two devices seeing one drawer needs the iCloud container on the team and an iCloud account, so it is a device test (`docs/device.md` §3.13). BEDIP-219.

**Sync.** The drawer's store is mirrored by SwiftData to a CloudKit **private** database, `iCloud.com.designedbybruno.stub`: the person's own iCloud, which only they can read. Nothing goes to a server of ours, because there is none; the promise in ADR-005 becomes "nothing reaches anyone but you". Photographs are external storage, so they travel as CloudKit assets. It is **on by default** and turned off in Settings › Stub › Keep in iCloud (`drawer.iCloud`, read at launch, so it takes effect the next time the app opens). The season sheet says where the drawer is kept. A build that cannot mirror (a profile without the container) opens the drawer on the phone alone and logs why; the tests never mirror (`Drawer.wantsSync` is false under XCTest). The app is entitled to the container, to CloudKit and to remote notifications, because CloudKit stops the app outright when it is asked for a container the build is not entitled to; the person's switch is the setting, not the build.

**The schema is versioned.** `StubSchemaV1` is the drawer as it was, frozen exactly, so a store made before today is recognised and migrated (`StubMigrationPlan`, lightweight); `StubSchemaV2` is what CloudKit can carry: every property optional or defaulted, and no unique attribute. `Stub.id`'s uniqueness moves into the app: `Drawer.dedupe` keeps the first copy of a stub filed (with a photograph, if any copy has one) whenever the drawer opens and whenever another phone's changes arrive (`NSPersistentStoreRemoteChange`). A test migrates a version 1 store with photographs and finds every stub with its id, photo and tilt; another opens the schema mirrored to CloudKit, which refuses a schema with a unique attribute or an undefaulted property (it did, for the version 1 shape, in the first test run). Every `ModelConfiguration` says `cloudKitDatabase` explicitly: with the entitlement, SwiftData's default is to mirror, and an in-memory test store mirrored is an exception on CloudKit's queue.

**What the press keeps travels with the stubs.** `PressRecord` (a kind, a key, bytes, when) holds each release's printed edition, its pulled proof, the proof on the press and the owner's signature. `Editions` and `Signatures` keep their fast local copies and write through to the ledger; `PressLedger.reconcile` makes the copies match the ledger when another phone's records arrive. The first time a phone joins, what it already had goes into the ledger without overwriting what another phone put there; from then on the ledger is the truth, so going back (deleting a proof) on one phone goes back on all of them. Whether a store's ledger has been filled is a marker record in the store itself, never a flag beside it: a store that is new, recreated or migrated without its records is filled again from the phone's copies before anything is adopted from it. (On 2026-10-13 a flag in `UserDefaults` outlived the simulator's records after an older build migrated the store back and the new one forward again, and the press adopted an empty ledger and lost its editions; a test now holds the marker to it, and fails without it.) The printed editions travel too, so the same release is the same card on every phone even when the model chose it on only one.

**Venues and circuits.** Each stub has an optional `venue` (the cinema normalised: "EVENT CINEMAS QUEEN ST" is "Event Cinemas Queen St") and `circuit` (a chain, "independent", or nil). They are derived on the phone: from a pass's issuer when there is one, else from the cinema the reader read, against a small lookup of New Zealand and Australian circuits and the independents the drawer has met (`Venue`). The model is not asked: a chain is a fact, and a model asked for one would guess. The raw `cinema` is kept and the person's correction of it would re-place it. Stubs from before are placed the first time the drawer opens. The season counts the chains and the independents apart, and the nights in the front row (row A); the sentence can say "and three circuits", and the season sheet lists them quietly.

**Why.** A record that lives on one phone dies with it, and a private archive that cannot survive a lost phone is a diary written in sand. Private CloudKit is the only sync that keeps the promise: the person's account, Apple's encryption, no server of ours to trust or to breach. On by default because the risk the drawer is protecting against (losing it) is the one people do not think to protect themselves from; off is one switch, and the sheet says which it is.

**Consequences.** A new property on `Stub` is optional or defaulted and arrives in a new schema version with a migration stage; never a unique attribute. The production CloudKit schema has to be deployed from the CloudKit console before a TestFlight or App Store build syncs (development builds create the development schema on first sync). The widget opens the store read-only and unmirrored. A new circuit is a line in `Venue.circuits` with a test.

## ADR-025 — Keep a stub opens the camera

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built and proved in the simulator with a photograph standing in for the camera (`-camera file`). A real camera, the torch and the permission prompt are device tests (`docs/device.md` §3.1).

Keep a stub (the dock, the empty drawer, "Log a stub" from Siri) opens a full-screen viewfinder, not a form. Four registration marks, the press's own, rest where a landscape ticket goes; Vision looks for a ticket ten times a second (`VNDetectRectanglesRequest`, aspect 0.25 to 1), the marks spring to its corners, and when three looks in a row agree they ink orange with a firm tap: it has seen it. ## ADR-026 — The cabinet: years as folders, stubs in the order you put them, drawers you make

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built, tested (`CabinetTests`) and used in the simulator, including holding a stub and letting it go on a drawer's label.

**Order.** Every drawer is filed by year, newest first: that order is the drawer's and nobody moves a night into another year. Within a year the person puts the stubs where they like: hold one and it comes up off the table; the stubs of its year make room as it goes; let it go and it stays. Until a year is touched it stands newest night first, and a stub kept later stands at the front of its year. The place is `Stub.handOrder`, written only for the stubs that moved, so a sync carries only those. VoiceOver moves a stub one place at a time ("Move earlier in 2025").

**Years are folders.** Each year sits behind a manila folder's tab, the tabs stepping left, middle, right as a box of folders is cut. Tap the tab and the year's stubs slide down into the folder and its front comes up over them with a thud; closed, the tops of the tickets stand out of it and the front says how many and which months. Tap again and it opens with a lighter tap. Which years are closed is kept per drawer.

**Drawers.** The drawer of every stub is the one the app opens on. The person can make others ("Festival 2025", "With Mum"); each is a brass label on the same shelf, swiped along, the next ones showing at the edges and stepping back as they leave the middle; a blank label at the end names a new one. A drawer holds stubs by id, in the person's order, filed by the night they were seen; a stub can be in several, as a card catalogue cross-files a book, and is never anywhere but the drawer of every stub too. Hold a stub and the cabinet's other labels come down from the top: let it go on one and it is filed there, on the blank one and a new drawer is named with it in, on "Take it out" and it leaves the open drawer. The same is a list (Choose, beside the faces) and a menu (File, under each card) for anyone who would rather tap. Keep a stub while a drawer is open and it is filed there as well. Renaming and taking a drawer out are on its season sheet; its stubs stay.

**Tickets or editions.** The drawer shows each stub as the ticket it was or as its edition, the person's choice, kept. Switched, the cards turn over in a wave, front to back, each a quarter-turn away, the face swapped edge-on and a spring back to flat. Editions the press has not printed yet are printed in turn while the drawer shows editions. Every card in a row sits in the same mount, as tall as the row's tallest ticket, its words hanging from the same line, so the drawer reads in rows however mixed the tickets are.

**The store.** `StubSchemaV3`: version 2 frozen, plus `Stub.handOrder` and `StubDrawer` (an id, a name, a position, the stubs' ids). Lightweight from 2; still what CloudKit can carry (defaults, nothing unique, no relationships: a drawer keeps ids, so a stub deleted on another phone is skipped, not dangling). A test migrates a version 2 store with a record and a stub and files it in a new drawer.

**Why.** Bruno: hold to reorder, file into drawers, and logic and order that scales over years without losing the essence. Years are the order a dated archive already has and the one a person reaches for ("that was 2024"); a hand order within them gives control without letting the drawer lose its spine. The two uneven columns of Day 1 read as a pinboard at ten stubs and as noise at a hundred; a grid of mounts under year folders is a cabinet at any size, and the tilt and the deal keep it the same table. Drawers are the person's own taxonomy without tags or a settings screen, made by the gesture that moves a stub anyway.

**Consequences.** DESIGN.md rule 4 is superseded: two even columns of mounts, by year. A new way to group stubs is a drawer, not a filter. Anything that lists stubs in order uses `Cabinet.order`.

## ADR-027 — The widget is the edition, drawn from its marks

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built and used in the simulator on the home screen (medium: dealt, turned, opened). The lock-screen families and the tinted and clear home screens are device checks (`docs/device.md` §3.3).

The widget ("The drawer") deals one stub at a time, newest kept first, and draws its **edition**, not the photograph: the same `Composition` and `Printer` the app prints with, compiled into the extension (everything of the edition but the light, the stock's shaders and the back). Medium and large show the card on the paper with the film, the night, the seat and the cinema beside it, "2 of 11", and two buttons that run in the widget (App Intents): **Turn**, which shows the ticket as it was kept, and **Another**, which deals the next. Small shows the poster whole on its own ground. Tapping it opens that stub (`stub://stub/<id>`). The deck's place is kept in the App Group and starts again when a stub is kept. The edition shown is the press's newest record of it with the pulled proof over it, from the drawer's store, so a proof pulled on another phone is on this widget too. On a tinted home screen the stock drops away and the first plate and the foil take the tint.

**Why.** Bruno's call: the designed card has better iOS support than a photograph. A photograph in a widget is a bitmap at one size, tinted to mud; an edition is vector marks, sharp at every size, with real layers the system can tint. And the widget becomes a way to look through the drawer, not a frame on one stub.

**What the widget host cannot do.** A widget is drawn by the system from an archive the extension makes, and two things in the app's drawing do not survive it. The app's `TicketShape` is not a shape the archive knows, so the outline is handed over as a path, computed in the extension. And the system cannot draw the app's variable faces from inside a `Canvas`: Noir's title in Newsreader italic, set on a canvas, made the whole timeline "bad data" and the widget blank, while the same face as `Text` drew. So in the widget the marks are canvases and the lines of type are `Text`, set at the same baselines, widths and turns (`Printer.setsWords = false`). Found by bisecting on the home screen; the sample edition (blueprint, mono only) had rendered all along.

**Consequences.** The edition's composition must stay free of Metal and of the app's views (`BackLayout` holds the back's arithmetic for the punches; `Release.stripFormats` moved out of the parser). A new mark shape needs drawing in `Printer` only; a new face needs no change, because the widget sets type as text. The last-stub widget's kind is kept, so a widget already placed becomes this one.

## ADR-028 — Your card, and the icon drawer

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built and used in the simulator (choosing an icon, the drawer locked and open). The purchase needs the in-app purchase in App Store Connect and the Paid Apps agreement (BEDIP-217); it is tested against `Stub.storekit` when run from Xcode.

**No account.** Stub has nothing to sign in to: the drawer is the person's, on the phone and in their own iCloud (ADR-024), and an account would be a server of ours to hold a password for nothing. What a person needs in its place is somewhere that says whose this is and holds the few settings, and makes the app feel kept rather than anonymous. That is **Your card**, a reader's card of the kind a library gives, opened from a brass-ringed button in the top corner above the labels (the place a phone keeps the person's own things): the name typed on it if they give one (it syncs, as a record beside the stubs), the night the drawer starts, the stubs, cinemas and drawers, and the signature from the press if they have signed. Under it, in the card's own words and no boxes: **The icon**; **The drawer** (tickets or editions, keep it in my iCloud, the drawer as a spreadsheet); **Stub** (the privacy promise in one sentence, the site, the version).

**The icon drawer.** Ten icons were drawn for the app (`scripts/make-icons.swift`, all CoreGraphics in the app's faces, from fixed seeds): Torn, Roll, Label, Riso, Punched, Letterpress, Edition, Marquee, Hand, Scallop. Torn is the app's own. The other nine are alternate icons, chosen in Your card once the person has opened the icon drawer: one non-consumable purchase, `com.designedbybruno.stub.icons`, about five dollars (US$4.99 in the test store; the tier is set in App Store Connect), Family Sharing on, restorable, no subscription. StoreKit 2 checks the phone's own record of it when the card opens and listens for changes. Nothing else is for sale and nothing in the app needs it.

**Why.** Bruno: all ten, behind a small one-time payment, and an account section that legitimises the app without taking the stage. Icons are the right thing to charge for: wholly optional, about the person's own phone, and they cost nothing to keep working. The card is a reader's card because the app is an archive, and an archive's membership is a card, not a profile.

**Consequences.** BEDIP-217 gains: create the in-app purchase (non-consumable, that product id, a price near five dollars, a review screenshot of Your card) and sign the Paid Apps agreement. A new icon is a function in `make-icons.swift`, `--install`, and a name in `IconDrawer.icons`. A new setting goes on the card only if it is one a person would look for; there is still no settings screen.

## ADR-029 — Buttons: clean capsules, not stamps

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built and seen in the simulator. Supersedes the buttons of ADR-022.

Every button is a `StubButtonStyle` (`Stub/Design/Buttons.swift`): a capsule in near-black ink with parchment words for a screen's one primary act, a cream capsule with a hairline edge for a destination or a second act, words alone for what should not be shouted, and a rounded tile for a row of acts (the press, share, file under a card; the bench's choices). Host Grotesk Medium on all of them. Pressed, it goes into the page: 96.5 per cent of its size, its shadow drawn close, one soft tap at contact; let go, a spring brings it back with one small overshoot. Under Reduce Motion it does not move and darkens where it is pressed. Tickets or Editions is one segmented control, a cream track with an ink thumb that slides to the choice (`Segmented`). The camera's shutter is a paper disc in a paper ring, the ring taking the marks' orange when they have found a ticket. The widget's buttons are the cream capsule at its size.

**Why.** Bruno: the stamp and its animation (the ink a few points out of register over an orange or navy plate, printing into register under the thumb, lifting off with a tip, a ring of ink and a second tap) had stopped feeling right; he wanted clean, sleek, modern buttons that still felt like Stub. The misregistration was a good idea in the wrong place: it is what makes the prints and the editions feel printed, and repeated on every control it read as a costume. The inks, the faces and one spring with one overshoot are what make the buttons Stub's; the rest is quiet so the drawer, the cards and the press are what you notice.

**Consequences.** DESIGN.md rule 11 is rewritten. A new button is `.buttonStyle(.stub(...))`; a new choice of a few options is `Segmented`. The brass label holders and the card button keep their own presses, because they are objects, not buttons. The screenshots before this date show the stamps.

## ADR-030 — The card's acts are its gestures, said plainly; only the lever keeps a proof

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built and used in the simulator (a hold into the press, a change of inks, back, "Pull it and keep it", the card in its new inks). Supersedes the detail's row of acts (ADR-022).

**The card.** The row under the card (The press, Share, File, On the table) is gone. Share is in the top right, with a ⋯ menu beside it (take it to the press, turn it over, put it on the table). Filing is one quiet line under the cinema ("In With Mum", a menu). The card says under it, every time, what a finger does: "Tap to turn over · Hold for the press" on the front, "Tap to turn back · Hold the photo to lift the print" on the back. Holding draws a line of orange round the card's edge (`TicketOutline`, 0.55 s after a 0.12 s beat, so a tap never shows it); when the line closes the card lifts and goes to the press, and if the finger moves or lifts first the line runs back. Over the bed, whenever the proof on the press differs from the card: an orange dot, "Not kept yet", "Pull the lever to keep it", and an arrow to the lever; straight after a pull, "Kept. ## ADR-031 — The press's dial: named moves on a knob, and the parts named under the card

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built, tested (`PressTests` hold every move to the dependency rule) and seen in the simulator. Supersedes the 0–99 takes of ADR-016 for what a part's wheel does.

**What the dial does.** Turning a part used to roll it again: take 14 of the disc was the disc drawn from another fork of its die, a circle a little bigger and a little lower. Bruno found it underbaked: the dial "just moves stuff around a small grid", nothing a person would call a choice. The dial now has nine named moves, the same on every part of every movement: **As drawn**, **Larger**, **Smaller**, **Turned**, **Echo** (two fading repeats stepped out behind it, as a riso prints a plate twice off register), **Outline** (solid shapes cut to their edge), **Mirrored** (to the other side of the sheet), **Swapped** (its two inks traded) and **Redrawn** (the next roll of its die, the old idea kept as one move). Each is geometry (`Treatment.swift`): the part's own marks rewritten, never a transform laid over the drawing, so the printer, the widget, the hit test, the punches and the in-betweens read the result as they read any mark. A frame carries what hangs from it when it is made larger, smaller, turned or mirrored, so the title stays in its band; a frame moves less than a piece does. A proof's takes are now these moves; a take from before (up to 99) is clamped to the last.

**The dial.** A black knob standing on the bench with its shadow under it, a knurled grip whose sixty-four ridges are each lit by how squarely they face the light from the upper left (so the edge glints on one side and falls into shadow on the other as it turns), a glossy top with a highlight that stays where the lamp is, an orange pointer cut into it, a dimple for the finger. The nine moves are engraved round it on a 270° arc like a radio's dial, the chosen one in ink with an orange tick; tap a name and the knob turns to it. Turned by hand it clicks into each move at the stock's sharpness, resists past the ends, and springs to the nearest on release; the card redraws through the in-between.

**What can be touched.** Under the card, whenever nothing is in hand, the parts are named as chips, each with a dot of the ink it is printed in and its move if it has one ("Block · Larger"). A chip takes hold of the part as a tap on the card does; the registration marks still show where each is. The status line names the part and its move ("The block · Echo"), and the pencil on the back writes the moves ("disc echo · bars turned").

**Why.** A choice should be one a person can name and predict. Nine moves a printer would recognise make every turn a visible, different idea, and the same nine on every part means learning the dial once. The knob is an object because the press is a room of objects; the names are engraved on it because a dial with nothing on it is a slot machine.

**Consequences.** `docs/editions/edition.js` mirrors As drawn only; the goldens hold the rest of the poster to it when a part is redrawn. A new move is a case in `Treatment` and a branch in `Composer.treated`, with `partsAreIndependent` holding it to the rule.

## ADR-032 — Patina from time and from hands, and the card says what its marks mean

**Date:** 2026-10-15 · **Status:** decided; built, tested and seen in the simulator (`-age 6 -viewings 2 -handled 40`). Refines brief §6.2 and Day 28.

**What changed.** The back of a punched, aged card showed a crescent and an orange dot, and nothing said why (Bruno: "not really clear what these marks mean"). Now: the paper warms most at its edges, the way paper in a drawer tones from the outside in, rather than taking a yellow cast; foxing is a few small sepia clusters (a lead speck and two beside it) after two years, not a rust spot after one; and hands wear a card too. Each time a stub's card is opened it is counted (`Handling`, on this phone), and the count rubs its corners light and burnishes a patch of sheen where a thumb holds it, which only shows as the light moves across it. A punch reads as a hole: the card's shadow falls into it from the side the light comes from and the cut edge catches the light on the other (`PunchWalls`). And the detail says it, under the machinery lines: "Punched once, as a conductor punches a ticket: this is your second viewing. 6 years since the night, the paper warming at its edges and foxed in places; taken out 40 times, its corners softened where a thumb goes."

**Why.** Patina is the app's argument that a kept thing gains rather than decays; marks that look like glitches argue the opposite. Toning at the edges, small clustered foxing and a burnished corner are how paper actually ages, they are materials that answer the light rather than noise (DESIGN.md, no fake grain), and they are slow enough that a new card is pristine and a card from years ago is quietly lovely. Saying what they mean turns a mystery into a story the card tells about you.

**Consequences.** Handling is per phone and not synced: hands are local, and the same card can be more worn on the phone it is held on most. The patina's tests now hold "nothing in the first two years", "a few small clusters at most", and that hands wear but never fox.

## ADR-042 — The audit is judged by evidence; words are set level; nothing under 12 points

**Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Status:** decided; built; the audit runs in CI (`scripts/a11y.sh`). Product decision: docs/decisions/005.

**What changed.** `StubUITests/AccessibilityTests` audits ten screens at the default size and the largest accessibility size, with Increase Contrast off and on, and judges every issue Apple's `performAccessibilityAudit` reports instead of failing on the first. Four kinds are settled by evidence, narrowly, as Apple's session on audits shows (WWDC23 10035): printed artwork (`accessibilityIdentifier("artwork")` on an edition's two faces, a ticket's photograph and the camera's picture: hidden from VoiceOver, said by the card's label, and exempt from WCAG's contrast rule as text in a picture); contrast, which the test measures on the screen in WCAG's way and fails under 4.5:1; Dynamic Type, where a flagged label must stand at least 1.4 times as tall at the largest size, and a navigation bar's own buttons keep their size and show the Large Content Viewer; and text Vision reads in the pixels, which must each be an element's words, on a picture, or behind a half-height sheet. Everything else fails the run.

The audit found real faults, all fixed. The import form stayed reachable behind the camera; it is now removed while the camera is up. The press's part chips sat inside a status line that hid its children, so VoiceOver could not reach them. The press's "kept" line was an empty, unlabelled element when it had nothing to say, and it shrank its words. "Constructivist" broke mid-word on a small phone at the default size. The plates and the press cut words at the largest sizes. The season's year rows were too small to touch, and Your card squeezed its labels into a 58-point column at the largest sizes. The press now scrolls at the accessibility sizes, and its choices change shape rather than break a word. The plates grow as tall as their words need. Your card stacks its labels. The calendar is one element.

**Why.** Apple's contrast check failed text at 14:1 inside a sheet and passed a grey at 3.2:1 on its own: it misjudges text grouped with a background under any rotation, even 0°. The `-a11y-lab` sample sheet (`ContrastLab.swift`, Debug and Performance only) showed it. The type itself was never the fault.

**Consequences.** Words are set level (DESIGN.md rule 3): a stub's picture keeps its tilt, its caption does not, and Your card lies level. No interface words under 12 points (DESIGN.md rule 8). The season's figures are placed in turn with a render effect (`visualEffect`), not a layout one.

## ADR-043 — No two stubs alike: a later viewing pulls a variant, every copy is its own pull, the stage repeats nothing

**Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Status:** decided; built; tested (`VariantTests`). DESIGN.md, Editions rule 1.

**What changed.** Bruno: the collection should never feel repetitive; it should be a given that every stub is unique.
Three places repeated:

- **A film seen twice printed the same card twice.** Now `Edition.pulled(viewing:)` gives every viewing after the first
  a variant: the release's own drawing (movement, stock, seed and takes untouched) in its own inks. The ground is
  inverted first, so the variant reads as a variant at a glance, as a print studio's does. The rest follow in an order
  the release's seed decides (`Genome.variants`), and no two viewings share inks until all twelve palettes are used.
  It is applied where an edition is composed for a copy (`Composition`), so the drawer, the detail, the press, the
  share and the widget agree. The card's back, its spoken label and the detail's line say "A variant of …". Inks chosen
  at the press are a choice the variant never overrides (`Edition.inksChosen`, set by `applying`). On a later viewing,
  choosing the release's own inks is kept as a choice too.
- **Every copy of an edition was the same pull.** Now each plate lands where this copy's pull put it
  (`Composition.registration`): up to three quarters of a point for a colour plate, a quarter for the type, a third for
  the foil. The stock's fibre and sheen are the copy's (`Composition.paper`). Seeded by the release and the viewing, so a
  card never moves between looks.
- **The staged drawer cycled ten fixture photographs a year apart.** The same Brutalist ticket, seat H12, sat in 2026
  and in 2025, in every screenshot and every test. Now `Stage.fill` files each fixture once, then draws a ticket for an
  invented film at an invented cinema, each with its own night, seat, screen, price, paper and tilt. Past the list's
  end, a film comes back as its sequel. Invented, so a screenshot never carries a real name beyond the fixtures.

**Why.** Collecting is the point of a drawer, and a drawer of repeats is not a collection. A rewatch is the strongest
signal a person can give a film, and it now earns a card of its own, the way a print studio's variant is collected
beside the regular.

**Consequences.** The edition is still the film's and the same on every phone for a first viewing. A later viewing's
card is the same on every phone for that viewing number. `VariantTests` render two viewings and require that more than
half the card differs.

## ADR-044 — Each year turns over on its own, from a tab on its folder

**Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Status:** decided; built. DESIGN.md rule 4.

**What changed.** Bruno: the tickets and editions switch, floating over the drawer as one control for every year, felt
off; each year should flick between editions and stubs, integrated into the design, and the control should be larger
and more obvious while staying elegant. The drawer-wide picker is gone. Every year folder has a second tab at its right
end, always in the same place, under the thumb: its two sides as marks, a ticket lying on its side and a card standing up, each notched at its perforation (words
at first; Bruno asked for the marks), the side showing under the ink thumb the drawer's other choices use (ADR-029), each side a 44-point target that turns the year over when chosen. The year's tab
staggers between two positions so the two tabs never meet. At the accessibility sizes they stack. A flick across a
year's stubs turns it too. The flick reads the finger's predicted end, so a quick one counts. Its stubs turn one after
another from the first in the year, not from the top of the drawer. Each year remembers its side, per drawer
(`drawer.turnedYears`). A year turned back to the drawer's way forgets it was turned. The press prints missing editions
whenever any year shows them. `-face` sets every year one way, for screenshots and tests.

**Why.** A switch above the cabinet is the app's chrome; a tab on the folder is the cabinet's own. A person looks at a
year as a year: a festival's editions beside last winter's tickets.

## ADR-045 — Every edition has a hand: one master's move under the movement's drawing

**Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Status:** decided; built; contact sheets checked by eye. DESIGN.md, Editions rule 9.

**What changed.** Bruno: push the editions' quality, style and individuality, with taste from designers such as Karel
Martens, without losing the movements or the press. A contact sheet of every movement showed the fault: within a
movement every film came out nearly the same, the hash changing a title and inks but hardly the composition. Each edition
now has a hand (`Hand`), drawn from the release's seed among the devices its movement admits: overprinted (Martens),
on the grid (Crouwel), in sequence (Aicher), at scale (Corita Kent, Paula Scher). The hand reads the release's own
drawing (takes left out, so turning a part never moves it), keeps clear of its type and its large forms, and is laid
over the field a movement lays first and under everything else. It multiplies into a light ground and screens onto a
dark one in the lighter ink. It is a declared part of every movement (`<movement>/hand`) that never turns. The colophon
names it ("Constructivist, overprinted, sand inks, …").

**Why.** A movement is a grammar; the hand is the decision a designer makes inside it. The first pass, checked on contact
sheets, overprinted rectangles into stray frames, scattered grid cells at random, and hid under full-bleed fields; the
second admits each device only where it survives the movement's grammar, echoes only discs and rings, and builds the grid's
monogram from a five-by-seven alphabet in the clear place nearest a seeded corner.

**Consequences.** `docs/editions/edition.js` draws the movements, not the hand; the goldens compare the movement's marks.
The masters are named here and in DESIGN.md, never on a card.

## ADR-046 — The press's studio: overprint and stamp the card; the dial is cut

**Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Status:** decided; built; tested (`StudioTests`). DESIGN.md › The press, rules 1, 2, 3, 5, 6. Supersedes ADR-031's dial.

**What changed.** Bruno: the turn wheel was a poor execution of what customising could be: it sent single parts to
individual options that ruined the design. Think out of the box; take the cues from his taste (his reference board:
Rauschenberg's silkscreen layers, Karel Martens's overprints, risograph halftones, vintage luggage tags, rubber date
stamps, postmarks and cancellations) with universal product appeal. The dial, its registration marks and the part chips
are gone. In their place the bench holds the studio's two tools:

- **Overprint.** Drag a finger across the card and a pass is pulled through the screen on the tray: three discs along
  the stroke overprinting one another (Martens), a dot screen swelling where the stroke ends, bands stepping toward the
  ground (Aicher), or the title's first letter enormous (Corita Kent). The first screen on the tray, wherever there is
  a photograph, is **the stub itself**: the ticket's own photograph pulled through as a silkscreen transfer, its darks
  printing in the ink, its paper left as card, in the image layer over the plates and under the type (Rauschenberg). It
  makes every card carry the one thing no other card has. The live pass and a squeegee blade follow the finger before it
  lands.
- **Stamp.** Tap and a rubber stamp of the night lands where the finger was: the date, Admit one, the seat in a ring,
  the viewing between rules, the cinema's postmark with its cancellation. Each is pressed a few degrees crooked (the
  same each time for the same place) and kisses the card a second, fainter time.

Every act prints in one of the card's three inks, chosen from pots on the tray, multiplied into a light card and screened
onto a dark one, over the drawing, in the order it was done. Stamps print the copy's own facts, so a second viewing's
card stamps its own date. Acts are kept in the proof (`Proof.acts`, optional so older proofs read), laid over the
composition as the movement's `studio` part, and named for VoiceOver ("Overprint across the card", "Stamp the card").
"Take it off" lifts the last act; every act can be undone. A stamp lands with a heavy knock, a pass with a lighter one.

**Why.** Customising a designed thing by changing its parts makes it worse in proportion to how much it was designed.
Acting on it the way printed ephemera are acted on (overprinted, stamped, cancelled) makes it more itself, and makes it
the person's. Every form is the edition's own grammar and every ink its own, so there is no wrong answer.

**Consequences.** `Proof.takes` and the part moves (`Treatment`) stay in the model: proofs pulled with them still print
and their pencil still reads. Nothing in the app sets them now.

## ADR-047 — Coated finishes at the press, and the studio made sure under a finger

**Date:** 2026-10-16 · **Status:** decided; built; tested (`EditionTests`, `StudioUITests`). DESIGN.md › Editions rule 3.

**What changed.** Bruno asked for more coated card options. The press's swatch book now carries four coated finishes
beside cotton, coated, foil and holographic. Each is a material that answers the light, never grain (DESIGN.md, Apps):
- **Gloss card**: a narrow bright highlight; the ink held proud and crisp.
- **Matte card**: no sheen; the ink sits flat and deep.
- **Soft-touch**: a broad breath of sheen, the impression deep, the finger dragging.
- **Pearl card**: mica in the coat, a long quiet wave of hue under the light; a hologram's rainbow bands were tried
  first and were too loud.

The stock shader gains two material parameters (`sheen`, how tight the coat's highlight is; `pearl`). The finishes are
the press's alone: `Genome.stocks` and the model's vocabulary stay the first four, frozen, so no edition already printed
changes its stock. The swatch book is a rail; a tap prints on a swatch; holding one lets it be felt.

Bruno also said the overprint did not work. A touch test (`StudioUITests`) drove it as a person would and found the
weak point: a quick stroke ending before the stroke was recorded was dropped, and the stroke shared the card with the
screen's other gestures. Now:
- the pass is committed from where the finger came down to where it lifted;
- the stroke has priority while the screens are out;
- a tap overprints too, a pass straight across where the finger came down;
- the press cannot be swiped away while a tool is out.

The test runs every act by touch: Overprint opens, a tap and a drag each pull a pass, Stamp stamps, a finish is chosen.

## ADR-049 — The film's own poster, printed in the movement's process

**Date:** 2026-10-17 · **Status:** decided; built; tested (`KeyArtTests`); dormant until a TMDB token is in the build
and Bruno has decided the privacy label (decision 009). DESIGN.md › Editions rule 10.

**What changed.** Bruno: the edition should use elements from the film's poster, so each card holds up as its own
artefact, without losing the app's point of view. The official poster comes from The Movie Database (Bruno chose TMDB,
licensed). It is never pasted on. It is printed into the edition the way the movement prints:
- **Where:** the movement's own hero. `KeyArtWindow.find` takes the disc, block or torn shape that shows most once
  everything printed after it is down, shaped to hold a picture (no longer than about twice its width), never on the
  type plate and never holding words. A hero that holds words (deco's sun, with the year in it) becomes the picture's
  centre: the poster fills the field behind the sunburst. With no hero, the scale hand's enormous initial holds it, cut
  to the glyph's own outline (`KeyArtWindow.outline`). Then the tallest band of the field no type crosses; then, for a
  letterpress under a long title, the whole poster pressed blind into the cotton.
- **When in the run:** in the hero's own layer (`KeyArtWindow.layer`), so whatever the movement prints after the hero
  prints over the picture too, and with that plate's registration.
- **How:** the movement's screen (`KeyArtScreen`), made once a card by Core Image at three pixels to the point
  (`KeyArtPrints`). Swiss gets an enlarged 45° halftone; constructivist a line screen up its own diagonal; deco rings
  radiating from its sun; cutout a hard cut, as Saul Bass cut paper; riso a coarse 15° dot; letterpress and blueprint
  hatching; noir a horizontal line screen, its venetian blind.
- **In what ink:** the edition's own. The darks go in the darker ink on a light hero and the lights in the lighter ink
  on a dark one, with a second pull in the second ink 1.4 pt off register. A field takes the movement's colour at
  three fifths, so the type stays the strongest thing on the card. A letter takes its own ink, under itself.
- **Which picture:** Vision's attention saliency finds the poster's subject (`Posters.focus`), and the poster is scaled
  so the subject fills the window. The billing block never prints.

**The data.** `Posters` sends TMDB the title the ticket printed (formats stripped) and the year it was seen, once per
release, over an ephemeral session (no cookies, no identifier). It keeps the poster and its focus in Application Support,
and a release TMDB has no poster for is not asked about again for 30 days. Your card has the choice ("Print each film's
poster", on by default), "Forget the posters", and TMDB's notice. The Stub section's privacy line says the titles go to
TMDB whenever they can. With no token in `Config/Secrets.xcconfig` (gitignored; `Secrets.example.xcconfig` is
committed), `Posters.available` is false, no request is ever made, and none of this shows.

**Tried and dropped.**
- The poster's darks as a plain tonal mask in every movement: it read as a photo filter, the same on every card.
- Bands as windows: a 840 × 90 pt band showed 3% of the poster, a flat gradient.
- The picture above every plate: it covered the movement's own later shapes (the constructivist discs).

**Cost.** Only the last four posters are held decoded; the rest wait on the phone until a card prints them. The prints
are kept up to 48 MB, least recently seen let go first. `PerformanceTests/testDrawerScrollEditionsWithPosters` flings 60
editions each printing its poster under the editions' own ceilings: 0.403 s of CPU against the plain editions' 0.370 s
(≤ 0.9 s), on the simulator lane. The accessibility audit runs with the stand-in poster on every staged screen: clean.

**Debug.** `-poster poster-orchard.jpg` stands a fictional poster (`scripts/make-posters.py`) in for every film, so the key
art is built and screenshotted without TMDB or anyone's artwork in the repo. `-proof "movement=…"` shows it in each
movement.

## ADR-050 — The poster comes from the cinema

**Date:** 2026-10-17 · **Status:** decided by Bruno (decision 009, choice 4); built; tested (`AddLinkTests`,
`PosterSupplyTests`); driven end to end on the simulator. Supersedes ADR-049's source; its printing stands.

**What changed.** The Add to Stub link (ADR-021) takes a `poster` field: the address of the cinema's own poster for the
film. The cinema already holds the distributor-licensed artwork, so the poster is licensed where it comes from. No
title goes to anyone, and the App Store label stays **Data Not Collected**. TMDB stays in the code, off: it asks only in
a build with its token, and none has one.

**The address** (`AddLink.posterURL`): https on the standard port to a public name. No address in numbers, no
`localhost` or local-network name, no user or password, a path to a file, at most 2,048 characters. Its query and
fragment are dropped, so the address is the same for everyone handed it and can't carry anything about the person. A bad
address is left out (`unread: poster`) and the ticket still opens.

**The fetch** (`Posters.supplied`): never to open the ticket. The import sheet says "keeping it fetches the poster
once, from <host>", and the fetch happens on Keep. It runs over an ephemeral session with no cookies and no cache.
Redirects are followed only to an address that meets the same rule. The response must be an image of 8 MB or less, by
its declared length and again as it arrives. A release keeps the first poster it is handed. Nothing is fetched when
posters are off in Your card.

**What is kept** (`Posters.sanitised`): only an image that decodes, is no more than 60 megapixels, is poster-shaped
(width over height between 0.45 and 1.1) and is at least 400 pixels on its long side. So a tracking pixel, a banner or a
page is refused. It is written again as a JPEG of 900 pixels, with no metadata, under 400 KB. It is kept in the press's
ledger (`PressLedger.Kind.poster`), so it reaches the person's other phones through their own iCloud and outlives the
cinema's copy. The ledger now fetches its records' names and dates only, so a reconcile doesn't read every poster's
bytes.

**Your card.** "Print each film's poster" is shown always, and says where posters come from. "Forget the posters" is
gone: a cinema's poster is part of the keepsake and may not be fetchable again. TMDB's notice and the privacy line's
mention of TMDB appear only in a build with TMDB's token.

**CI, the same day.** The new fling with posters never measured on the runner: XCTest timed out reading the screen. Under
`-poster`, all sixty cards prepared the stand-in at once (sixty decodes, sixty saliency passes) and every print ran
together, and on the runner's emulated graphics processor the drawer stopped answering. Now the stand-in is prepared
once, prints go through one `Pressman` at a time and are skipped for a card already flung past, and a poster's subject
found on one phone is kept beside it.

**Debug.** `-link "<Add to Stub link>"` hands a link over two seconds after launch. Driven on the simulator with the
stand-in poster served from this repository on GitHub: the sheet named the host, Keep fetched it once, and the
blueprint edition printed it as it arrived.
